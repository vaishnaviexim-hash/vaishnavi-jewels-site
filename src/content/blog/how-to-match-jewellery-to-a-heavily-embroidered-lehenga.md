With a heavily embroidered lehenga, choose one hero piece of jewellery, usually the necklace, and keep everything else in proportion. Echo one stone colour from the embroidery, match the metal to the zari or thread, and skip extra layers where the work is heaviest. The aim is a look where the outfit and the jewellery support each other instead of competing.

## Why does heavy embroidery change the jewellery choice?

A lehenga covered in zardozi, resham, dabka or stone work is already full of detail and shine. If the jewellery is just as busy, the eye has nowhere to rest, and in photographs the necklace can disappear into the blouse. Rich, all-over work, the kind of heritage look that designers like Sabyasachi made popular, needs jewellery with a clear shape and a little breathing room.

So the question is not "how much jewellery?" but "which piece leads?"

## Which piece should be the hero?

Look at where the embroidery is heaviest.

- **Heavy blouse and neckline:** let the earrings and maang tikka lead, and choose a necklace that sits above the neckline rather than on top of the work, such as a choker.
- **Heavy skirt, lighter blouse:** the necklace can lead. A layered Polki set or a diamond collar with a long rani haar fills the open space well.
- **Heavy dupatta over the head:** the face is framed by fabric, so earrings and a tikka become more important than a long necklace that the dupatta will cover.

Once you know the hero, keep the other pieces one step quieter in size and colour.

## How do I match colours and metal to the embroidery?

1. **Match the metal to the thread.** Gold zari suits yellow-gold Polki and diamond settings. Silver or champagne thread work suits white or rose settings.
2. **Echo one colour, not all of them.** If the embroidery has red, green, pink and gold, pick one, often green emerald or a deep red stone, and repeat it once in the necklace or earrings.
3. **Use white to calm it down.** Diamonds, white Polki and pearls add light without adding another colour.
4. **Avoid large coloured stones that repeat the fabric.** A big stone in the same shade as the lehenga tends to vanish against it.

## How many pieces is too many?

For the ceremony, a typical balanced set is a necklace (or a choker plus one long necklace), earrings, a maang tikka or matha patti, bangles or kadas and a ring. Beyond that, ask whether each extra piece will be seen. A second long necklace over a heavily worked blouse, for example, often adds weight without adding anything to the photographs.

A quick check before the day: wear the full outfit with the jewellery and take a photo from a few steps away. If your face is not the first thing you notice, remove one piece.

## How do I keep it comfortable through long functions?

Heavy outfits are already tiring to wear for hours. Jewellery should not add strain:

- Ask for earrings with a sahara chain or ear support if they are long.
- Check that the necklace clasp does not catch on the embroidery at the back.
- Make sure bangles slide on and off easily over the chooda.

## Frequently asked questions

### What jewellery suits a velvet or deep jewel-tone lehenga for a winter wedding?

Warm gold, Polki and uncut diamonds, or emeralds and pearls that pick up the outfit colour, suit velvet and jewel tones well. Keep one strong necklace and let the velvet stay the backdrop.

### Polki or diamond jewellery: what should I wear for each wedding function?

Polki usually suits the wedding ceremony with a heavy lehenga, diamonds suit the reception in a lighter outfit, and lighter pieces of either work for the sangeet and mehendi. Choose one hero necklace per function.

### How do I get a layered royal bridal look without looking too heavy?

Build the look in steps: a tikka or matha patti, one layered necklace instead of several separate ones, and matching kadas. Space the layers so each one shows, and ask for lighter construction.
